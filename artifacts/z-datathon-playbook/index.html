01

What actually happened in past editions

This datathon has run since 2022 under IBM and the Shooting Stars Foundation, mostly on Devpost until this year's move to Hack2Skill. Public submission galleries and IBM's own case studies give a real, if partial, record of what got built and what got singled out.

Read this before you rely on any of it: official 2026 rules for team size and the exact prize split aren't published yet. The rubric and prize structure below are from the most recently completed edition — confirm both once the Hack2Skill registration page opens.

02

Reading between the winners

Only a handful of projects are explicitly tagged as winners in the public record, so treat this as directional, not statistical. But a pattern holds up across every source I found.

What blends in

  • Another classifier on a public Kaggle dataset (breast cancer, diabetes, heart disease — judges see several of these every year)
  • A multi-model "bake-off" with no clear winner among the models and mediocre accuracy (50s–60s%)
  • IBM Z mentioned once in a slide, never touched in the actual build
  • A notebook with no working demo — just plots and a printed accuracy score
  • A vague beneficiary: "this helps people with X" with no number attached

What stood out

  • One narrow, named problem with a real local dataset (Berkeley crime + street lights, Bengaluru lake imagery)
  • A single strong result stated as a number (97.4% accuracy; a working navigation output)
  • Actually deployed on LinuxONE — containerized, running, screenshot-able
  • Hit a platform-specific snag and solved it (the big-endian PyTorch story) — proof of real engagement with Z, not just Python-in-the-cloud
  • Small, focused teams that shipped something complete over something ambitious but half-built

The healthcare-classifier space (cancer, autism, mental health, genetic disorders, medical transcripts) was by far the most repeated category across every gallery I could pull. It's not a bad domain — two of the three confirmed winners are in it — but you're competing against the largest cluster of similar entries there. Environment/civic entries (Get Home Safe, lake encroachment, EV range, clean-energy pricing) were rarer and, going by IBM's own case study, get more attention when they're well executed.

03

The three 2026 tracks, and where IBM Z actually helps

The 2026 themes are a real shift from "run ML on Z" toward the platform's actual enterprise selling points: on-chip real-time inference, and hardware-level encryption. Building toward those specifically — even if you're simulating the hardware story rather than running on literal z16/z17 silicon — is the single biggest lever you have on the "use of IBM Z" score.

1 · Real-Time AI for Critical Decisions

IBM Z's actual headline AI story: the Telum on-chip accelerator does in-transaction fraud scoring at massive scale with near-zero added latency. This theme is IBM asking you to reenact that pitch in miniature.

  • APoint-of-transaction fraud/anomaly scoring on a simulated payment stream — closest literal match to Z's real flagship use case.
  • BICU vitals early-warning: streaming heart-rate/SpO2/BP data flags deterioration risk before a clinical event.
  • CFlood / disaster early-warning from streaming sensor + weather dataStrongest fit — river-gauge and rainfall telemetry scored in real time to trigger evacuation alerts before flooding hits. Chennai's monsoon flooding is a well-documented, recurring local crisis — this lands the "value to community" criterion hard for a judging panel with a Chennai hub, and the real-time/low-latency framing is a clean match for the theme.

2 · AI Secured: Innovation Without Exposure

Z's other real differentiator: pervasive encryption and IBM Secure Execution let you compute on sensitive data without ever exposing it in the clear — even to the infrastructure operator. This theme is that pitch, restated as a hackathon prompt.

  • AFederated learning across simulated hospitals or banksStrongest fit — train one shared disease-risk or credit-risk model without any institution's raw records leaving its own server. Mature open-source tooling (Flower) makes this genuinely buildable in 24 hours, and the theme's own name — "innovation without exposure" — is almost a literal description of what federated learning does.
  • BHomomorphic-encryption diagnosis: run inference directly on encrypted patient data (e.g. with TenSEAL). Technically flashier, but real homomorphic encryption is slow and fragile to demo live in 24 hours — treat as a stretch goal, not the core deliverable.
  • CDifferential-privacy loan-risk scoring across simulated banks (e.g. with Opacus) — same spirit as A, financial-services framing instead of health.

3 · Wildcard: Beyond the Obvious

Organizers are explicitly asking for something the judges haven't already seen three times that day. Given how crowded the healthcare-classifier space already is, "beyond the obvious" for this event specifically means: not another diagnosis model.

  • AAn AI copilot for legacy COBOL / mainframe modernizationHighest novelty — an agent that reads legacy COBOL, explains what it does in plain English, and flags migration risk. Nothing in four years of public submissions attempted this, and it's a real pain point IBM sells an actual commercial product against (watsonx Code Assistant for Z) — which means an IBM judging panel will recognize it instantly as relevant rather than generic.
  • BExplainable misinformation/deepfake detector — extends the "fake news detector" pattern seen before by adding a citizen-facing "why was this flagged" explanation layer, which past entries in this space didn't have.
  • CAn accessibility copilot for one specific underserved group (e.g. sign-language-to-text triage assistant) — high value-to-community if scoped narrowly to one real user group rather than "accessibility" in the abstract.
04

If you want one answer

Build the federated-learning privacy demo (Theme 2, option A)

Across novelty, feasibility in 24 hours, and fit to what IBM Z is actually known for, this is the strongest single bet. The tooling (Flower, plus scikit-learn or a small PyTorch model) is mature enough that a team without deep ML experience can get a working three-node federated demo running well within a day. It's not the most crowded category — unlike a standalone health classifier — and its name is almost literally the theme's name, so you never have to work hard to justify the fit in your pitch. Layer in a one-slide explanation of how IBM Secure Execution/pervasive encryption would harden it further in production, even if you don't implement that part, and you've directly answered all three judging criteria: a named beneficiary (institutions that can't legally share raw data), real novelty relative to the submission pool, and an explicit tie to Z's actual security architecture.

If your team is stronger on data engineering than ML, the flood early-warning idea (Theme 1, option C) is the next-best pick — same logic, different theme, and it carries a sharper "value to community" story if any judges are local to Chennai.

05

Prerequisites

Set these up before the 24 hours start — every hour lost to account provisioning or environment setup during the datathon is an hour not spent building.

  • Team. Past editions ran solo through 6-person teams — 2026's exact cap isn't published yet, but 3–4 people covering ML, backend/deployment, and a presenter is a good default.
  • Hack2Skill / StarHacks registration, completed before Oct 1.
  • IBMid account — needed for IBM Z Xplore badges and LinuxONE Community Cloud access. Provisioning a free L1CC virtual server can take a few days; request it early, not on event day.
  • GitHub account, with Git basics (clone, commit, push, branches) comfortable for every team member.
  • Docker installed and understood at a basic level — most credible past submissions containerized their model before deploying to LinuxONE.
  • Python fundamentals — pandas for data handling, scikit-learn for classical ML, and at least one of TensorFlow/PyTorch/Keras.
  • A demo framework — Flask or Streamlit — so you finish with something a judge can click through, not just a notebook.
06

Four-week roadmap

Mapped to today's date. The single most common failure mode in the record above was discovering a platform compatibility problem (the big-endian PyTorch issue) far too late — this plan front-loads exactly that risk.

Week 1 · Sep 16–22

Register, provision, pick a theme

Register the team on Hack2Skill. Create an IBMid and request LinuxONE Community Cloud access immediately — provisioning lag is your biggest schedule risk. Work through the free IBM Z Xplore starter badges. Agree as a team on one theme and one idea from the list above.

Week 2 · Sep 23–29

De-risk the platform, not the model

The moment your L1CC instance is live, deploy a trivial "hello world" model to it in Docker — this is where you find architecture-compatibility issues (like the s390x/big-endian PyTorch problem Team Oasis hit) while you still have time to fix them. In parallel, learn the core tool for your theme: Flower for federated learning, or a simple simulated data-stream producer for real-time scoring. Source or simulate your dataset.

Oct 1 — Deadline

Registration closes

Confirm your team is fully registered before this date.

Week 3 · Sep 30–Oct 6

Build the MVP

Working model plus a bare-bones Flask/Streamlit front end, end to end, even if ugly. Attend any IBM orientation or SME sessions — they're also where you pick up hints about what the 2026 judges are specifically looking for. Write your "value to community" framing in one sentence and pressure-test it on someone outside the team.

Week 4 · Oct 7–13

Integrate and rehearse

Full end-to-end build on LinuxONE Community Cloud. Start drafting your submission write-up and a 2–3 minute pitch script. Do one full dry-run demo with someone who hasn't seen the project before.

Oct 14–16

Freeze scope

Stop adding features. Polish the demo path, prepare for the failure cases you'll hit live, and plan sleep/shift schedules for the 24-hour window.

Oct 17–18

Datathon

Protect a complete, working demo over an ambitious but unfinished one — every winning project in the record above was fully working, none were the most technically ambitious idea in their gallery.

07

Pitching against the actual rubric

Structure your submission and demo to answer each judging criterion explicitly and in this order — don't make the panel infer it.

  • Value to community — open with the named beneficiary and a number: how many people, what specific harm reduced or time saved. Not "this helps people."
  • Creativity / novelty — say out loud what makes this different from the obvious version of the idea — this is your chance to pre-empt the "another classifier" reaction.
  • Technical design / use of IBM Z — show it running on LinuxONE Community Cloud on screen. Name the specific Z capability your idea maps to (real-time inference at the transaction layer, pervasive encryption, Secure Execution) even where you've only simulated it.
  • Live demo over slides — every standout project in the record above had something a judge could actually click through.
